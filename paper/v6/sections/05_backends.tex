\section{Matrix-function-action backends}\label{sec:backends}

The common engine reduces a time step to analytic operator actions.  A backend
implements those actions for a particular representation of $L$ and may expose
additional operations such as a native combination action, a fused sum across
distinct right-hand sides, or a shifted solve.  Table~\ref{tab:backends}
summarizes the release realizations and the scope of their work diagnostics.

\subsection{Reference realizations}

\textbf{DiagonalBackend} evaluates each analytic function on supplied diagonal
entries and is useful when the linear operator is already diagonalized.  It is
used in the stiff-order experiments to remove iterative matrix-action error from
temporal refinement.  \textbf{DenseBackend} materializes small dense
matrix-function calculations and serves as a reference path; it is not intended
as the large-scale default.

A \textbf{full-Arnoldi} backend constructs an orthonormal Krylov basis and
approximates the action through the projected problem.  Its role is both
numerical and diagnostic: because it retains full orthogonalization, it gives a
transparent reference against which the more specialized Krylov backend and the
fusion transformation can be exercised.

\subsection{KIOPS-type actions}

The KIOPS-type backend follows the augmented-Krylov philosophy of evaluating
linear combinations of $\varphi$ functions through a projected exponential,
with adaptive substepping and incomplete
orthogonalization~\cite{GaudreaultRainwaterTokman2018}.  It is an independent
implementation from the published algorithmic description; the reference KIOPS
MATLAB source is not vendored or translated, which is why the backend is not
called KIOPS.  It records matrix-vector products, accepted and rejected internal
substeps, projected dimensions and action failures.  These diagnostics are used
as algorithmic work observables, not as a claim that one product has identical
wall cost across all backends.  Algorithm~919 is an important predecessor for
this action-centric view: a polynomial inhomogeneous linear problem can be
recast as the action of a combination of the exponential and $\varphi$
functions, precisely the operation required repeatedly by exponential
integrators~\cite{NiesenWright2012}.

\subsection{Real-Leja interpolation}

The real-Leja backend interpolates the coefficient function $C(z)$ of an action
directly on the scaled spectral interval $[ha,hb]$ by Newton interpolation at a
real Leja sequence~\cite{CaliariVianelloBergamaschi2004}, with the spectral
bounds supplied by the user or estimated by a Lanczos eigenvalue solver, a
stopping rule on the Newton increments, and a maximum degree of 80 by default.
There is no substepping in this path.  When the maximum degree is reached
before the stopping rule is met, the backend raises a convergence error, which
the adaptive solvers treat as a rejected step.  With tolerance $10^{-10}$ the
default degree covers scaled spectral widths of a few hundred: degree 69 is
used at width 240 on a dissipative operator and the cap is reached near width
300, while the canonical problem of Section~\ref{sec:timing} reaches width 53
and the broadband problem of Section~\ref{sec:broadband} width 210.  The
option \texttt{target\_width} belongs to an experimental augmented-exponential
path with substepping that the public coefficient action does not use, so the
target width recorded in the canonical campaign had no effect on its
computations.  The practical attraction of the realization is different from
Krylov: with reliable real spectral bounds, repeated sparse products and vector
recurrences avoid the growing orthogonalization cost of a Krylov basis, at the
price of a degree that grows with the spectral width.  Leja-based exponential
integration is also the basis of LeXInt~\cite{DekaEinkemmerTokman2023}; \pkg{}
does not import or copy LeXInt source.

The manuscript does not state that Leja is intrinsically faster than Krylov,
or vice versa.  The canonical timing experiment favours the KIOPS-type backend
in all six tested configurations, the broadband experiment of
Section~\ref{sec:broadband} shows the ordering reversing with the tolerance,
and the development records of the supplement contain further crossovers with
problem size and spectral width.

\subsection{SciPy hybrid and pure exponential actions}

The SciPy/KIOPS hybrid delegates suitable pure exponential actions to SciPy's
\texttt{expm\_multiply} path and uses the KIOPS-type machinery for the remaining
$\varphi$ combinations.  The SciPy exponential-action algorithm is itself based
on norm estimation and scaling ideas that avoid forming
$e^A$~\cite{AlMohyHigham2011}.  This backend is reported with a counting
caveat: the \pkg{} counter does not include the internal products performed by
SciPy.  For that reason hybrid counts are not used as a total-work ranking in
the canonical timing discussion.

\subsection{Automatic and calibrated dispatch}

\texttt{AutoBackend} is a dispatch policy, not another matrix-function
algorithm.  Its rule-based policy selects a diagonal realization for a
one-dimensional diagonal array, a dense realization for sufficiently small
dense matrices, real Leja for sufficiently large Hermitian sparse problems with
a moderate scaled spectral width, and the KIOPS-type backend otherwise.  The
size and spectral-width thresholds are exposed because the crossover is
machine-dependent.

The release also contains a calibrated selector interface that can be fitted to
benchmark data for a defined problem class, and the adaptive solvers preserve
its calibration record when they clone it.  This is intentionally a moderate
claim: calibration can encode a useful local decision rule, but the current
experiments do not establish universal optimality.  The canonical timing
campaign in Section~\ref{sec:timing} is a counterweight to any interpretation
that the selector must win because it is automatic: there it chooses real Leja
in every configuration, and Leja is the slower realization.

\subsection{Fusion of compatible actions}\label{sec:fusion}

Two fusion levels are useful.  First, multiple $\varphi$ terms acting on the
same vector can be represented as one \texttt{PhiCombination}.  Second, the
EGLM engine may need a sum
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j=1}^{q} C_j(hL)v_j,
  \label{eq:distinct-rhs}
\end{equation}
with distinct right-hand sides.  The fused operation is defined when all terms
share one scale $c$: with $C_j(z)=\sum_k a_{jk}\varphi_k(cz)$,
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j=1}^{q} C_j(hL)v_j=\sum_k \varphi_k(chL)\,b_k,\qquad b_k=\sum_{j=1}^{q} a_{jk}v_j,
  \label{eq:fusion-grouping}
\end{equation}
and a Krylov backend evaluates the right-hand side with one augmented
projection of the vectors $b_k$.  When the scales differ, or when a backend
offers no fused operation, the engine evaluates the terms independently
without changing method semantics.  The two paths are algebraically identical,
but their approximate realizations need not agree bitwise; the ablation of
Section~\ref{sec:fusionexperiment} reports the agreement actually obtained,
below $1.3\times10^{-15}$ in relative terms.

Fusion is therefore an optimization of the common action boundary rather than
a special Krogstad or PEC code path.  The ablation demonstrates a substantial
reduction in recorded products with both Krylov realizations, while also
showing why that reduction should not be relabelled as an equal wall-time
speedup.
